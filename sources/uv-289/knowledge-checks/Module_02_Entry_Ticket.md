## Question 1
**Question**: Your average velocity over the last three sprints in CTS-285 was 14 story points. You are now working solo in CSC-289 (no team). What is the best approach to capacity planning for Sprint 1?
**A)** Commit to 14 points since that was your proven velocity
**B)** Start conservatively (8-10 points) since you lack current velocity data for solo work, then adjust based on Sprint 1 actuals
**C)** Commit to 20 points to challenge yourself in a capstone course
**D)** Skip capacity planning since you already know your velocity
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Past team velocity does not directly transfer to solo work — starting conservatively and calibrating from actual Sprint 1 data is the professional approach to an unknown environment.

---

## Question 2
**Question**: What is the primary input for calculating velocity?
**A)** The number of hours worked during the sprint
**B)** The total story points of completed (done) user stories at sprint end
**C)** The number of commits pushed to the repository
**D)** The number of user stories started during the sprint
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Velocity measures completed story points — only stories that meet the Definition of Done count, not partial work, hours logged, or commit volume.

---

## Question 3
**Question**: Which of the following is the strongest sprint goal?
**A)** "Work on the project"
**B)** "Complete as many stories as possible"
**C)** "Deliver a functional user authentication flow so that a user can register, log in, and access protected routes"
**D)** "Finish the backend"
**Answer**: C
**Explanation**: A strong sprint goal is specific, measurable, and tied to user value — it tells you exactly what "done" looks like and why it matters.

---

## Question 4
**Question**: During backlog refinement, you realize a user story is too large to complete in one sprint. What should you do?
**A)** Assign it more story points and commit to it anyway
**B)** Split it into smaller, independently deliverable stories that each provide user value
**C)** Move it to "Won't Have" since it is too big
**D)** Assign it to the next sprint where you will have more time
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Stories that exceed sprint capacity should be split into vertical slices — each slice delivers a thin but complete piece of functionality that a user can interact with.

---

## Question 5
**Question**: When selecting stories for a sprint, which combination of factors should guide your decision?
**A)** Story point size only — pick the smallest stories first
**B)** Sprint goal alignment, backlog priority, team capacity, and dependencies
**C)** Whichever stories have the most acceptance criteria
**D)** Stories the product owner mentioned most recently
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Story selection is a multi-factor decision — the sprint goal provides focus, priority ensures you work on what matters most, capacity prevents over-commitment, and dependencies reveal sequencing needs.

**A)** 1 week
**B)** 2 weeks
**C)** 3 weeks
**D)** 4 weeks

**Explanation**: CSC-289 uses a 2-week sprint cadence (matching the module structure), providing enough time to deliver meaningful increments while maintaining regular feedback loops.

## Question 7
**Question**: A daily standup log entry should answer which three questions?
**A)** What did I learn? What will I study? What grade do I expect?
**B)** What did I accomplish yesterday? What will I work on today? Are there any blockers?
**C)** What features did I finish? How many points did I earn? When will I be done?
**D)** What did the team do? What should the team do? What is the team blocked on?
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: The three standup questions — accomplished, planned, blocked — provide a quick daily snapshot of progress and surface impediments early, before they become crises.

---

## Question 8
**Question**: What is the key difference between a Sprint Review and a Sprint Retrospective?
**A)** The review focuses on the product (demo working software); the retrospective focuses on the process (how the team worked)
**B)** The review is optional; the retrospective is required
**C)** The review happens before the retrospective, but they cover the same topics
**D)** The review is for the instructor; the retrospective is for the student
**Answer**: A
**Explanation**: Sprint Review = inspect the product ("What did we build?"). Sprint Retrospective = inspect the process ("How can we work better?"). Both are required every sprint.

---

## Question 9
**Question**: You committed to 12 story points in your sprint plan, but by mid-sprint you have only completed 3 points and have a major blocker. What is the most professional response?
**A)** Work overtime to catch up and avoid documenting the problem
**B)** Document the blocker in your standup, update the sprint plan to reflect revised scope, and communicate the impact in your sprint review
**C)** Abandon the sprint and start fresh next sprint
**D)** Change the story point values to make the numbers look better
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Transparency and adaptation are professional PM practices — document the blocker, revise the plan, communicate honestly, and analyze what happened in the retrospective.

---

## Question 10
**Question**: Backlog refinement (grooming) should happen:
**A)** Only at the beginning of the project
**B)** Continuously — reviewing, re-estimating, splitting, and re-prioritizing stories throughout the project
**C)** Only when the product owner requests it
**D)** Only during sprint planning meetings
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Refinement is an ongoing activity — as you learn more about your project, stories need updating, splitting, re-estimating, and re-prioritizing to keep the backlog healthy and ready for planning.

---

## Question 11
**Question**: You are planning Sprint 1 and have 15 user stories in your backlog. You estimate your capacity at 10 story points. Which approach to story selection demonstrates the best planning?
**A)** Select stories totaling exactly 10 points regardless of priority
**B)** Select the highest-priority stories that align with the sprint goal, totaling no more than 10 points, and identify which stories to pull in if you finish early
**C)** Select 15 points of stories to ensure you stay busy the entire sprint
**D)** Select only 5 points of stories to guarantee you finish everything
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Good planning selects by priority and goal alignment within capacity, with stretch stories identified — this balances ambition with realistic commitment.

---

## Question 12
**Question**: In your async standup, you write: "Worked on stuff. Will keep working. No blockers." What is wrong with this entry?
**A)** Nothing — it answers all three questions
**B)** It lacks specificity — a useful standup names what was accomplished, what specific task is next, and honestly assesses blockers
**C)** It is too short — standups should be at least 500 words
**D)** It should include story point updates
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Vague standups provide no visibility into progress or problems — specific entries like "Completed login form validation (Issue #12), will start API endpoint for registration (Issue #14), blocked on database schema decision" are actionable.
