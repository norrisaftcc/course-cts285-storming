## Question 1
**Question**: You have a user story that depends on a design asset from your GRD-242 designer. The asset is not yet delivered. How should this story appear on your sprint board?
**A)** Remove it from the sprint entirely until the asset arrives
**B)** Mark it as a "Waiting on Design" blocked story with a clear dependency note, and continue working on unblocked stories
**C)** Mark it as complete and add the design later
**D)** Contact the designer every hour until they deliver
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Blocked stories should remain visible on the board with clear dependency labels — this maintains transparency and allows you to track how long dependencies remain unresolved.

---

## Question 2
**Question**: You need to give feedback on your designer's first icon draft. The icons are well-designed but use inconsistent stroke weights (1px, 1.5px, and 2px across the set). Using the SBI feedback model, which response is most effective?
**A)** "The icons are bad. Fix the lines."
**B)** "Situation: In the icon set you delivered Tuesday. Behavior: I noticed the stroke weights vary — home icon is 1px, settings is 1.5px, and notification is 2px. Impact: Inconsistent weights will look unpolished in the UI. Could you standardize to 1.5px across all icons?"
**C)** "Looks great, no changes needed!" (even though there is an issue)
**D)** "I'll just fix the stroke weights myself in the SVG code."
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: The SBI model (Situation, Behavior, Impact) delivers specific, objective, respectful feedback that gives the designer clear context and a concrete path to resolution.

---

## Question 3
**Question**: What does the SBI feedback model stand for?
**A)** Summary, Budget, Implementation
**B)** Situation, Behavior, Impact
**C)** Scope, Backlog, Integration
**D)** Sprint, Burndown, Iteration
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: SBI stands for Situation (when/where), Behavior (what specifically happened), Impact (the effect) — it separates observation from judgment and keeps feedback constructive.

---

## Question 4
**Question**: Your communication protocol with your GRD-242 designer should include:
**A)** Only your email address
**B)** Preferred contact method, expected response time, meeting schedule, and feedback format
**C)** A statement that you will only communicate through the instructor
**D)** Your phone number and a note to "text anytime"
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: A complete communication protocol sets mutual expectations — both parties know how to reach each other, how quickly to expect responses, when meetings happen, and how feedback will be delivered.

---

## Question 5
**Question**: Your designer delivers logo files as high-resolution PNG only. Your design brief specified SVG as the required format. What is the most professional response?
**A)** Accept the PNGs and try to convert them yourself
**B)** Acknowledge the work, explain why SVG is needed (scalability for favicon through splash screen without pixelation), and ask if they can export from their source file as SVG
**C)** Reject the files without explanation and wait for the correct format
**D)** Complain to the GRD-242 instructor about the designer
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Professional dependency management means acknowledging effort, re-explaining the technical rationale (not just restating the requirement), and requesting the specific format — most designers can re-export from their source files.

## Question 6
**Question**: You are planning Sprint 3, which overlaps with your Week 6 design brief presentation and the start of active GRD collaboration. How should you account for this in capacity planning?
**A)** Plan the same velocity as Sprint 2 since nothing has changed
**B)** Reduce coding story points to account for presentation prep, collaboration meetings, and feedback cycles — then add collaboration-specific tasks to the sprint plan
**C)** Skip sprint planning this sprint since the presentation is more important
**D)** Double your story points since you will have a designer helping
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Cross-team collaboration consumes real capacity — presentation prep, meetings, feedback reviews, and iteration cycles are work that must be planned for, not treated as free additions to an unchanged sprint.

## Question 7
**Question**: Your designer sends initial concepts that look different from what you envisioned. The designs are professional and well-executed but use a color palette you did not expect. What should you do?
**A)** Reject the designs immediately and insist on your original vision
**B)** Evaluate the designs against your brief's requirements and acceptance criteria — if they meet the functional needs and the designer can articulate their rationale, consider whether their expertise improves the project
**C)** Accept whatever the designer sends without review
**D)** Redesign everything yourself using the designer's tools
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Designers bring visual expertise developers typically lack — evaluate against requirements, not personal preference, and remain open to professional creative decisions that improve the project.

## Question 8
**Question**: An iteration cycle in cross-team collaboration typically follows which pattern?
**A)** Designer delivers final assets on the first attempt with no revision needed
**B)** Designer delivers draft, developer provides structured feedback, designer revises, developer reviews — repeated until acceptance criteria are met
**C)** Developer creates the designs and sends them to the designer for approval
**D)** Both parties work independently and compare results at the end
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Iteration cycles involve delivery, feedback, revision, and review — expecting perfection on the first draft is unrealistic, and structured feedback cycles produce better outcomes than working in isolation.

---

## Question 9
**Question**: You and your designer disagree about whether an illustration should be photorealistic or flat/minimalist. Your brief did not specify a style. What is the best way to resolve this?
**A)** Insist on your preference since you are the developer
**B)** Discuss the trade-offs together — consider the target audience, application tone, and technical constraints (file size, rendering) — and reach a joint decision documented in your communication log
**C)** Ask the instructor to decide for both of you
**D)** Use both styles in different parts of the application
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Professional conflict resolution means discussing trade-offs based on project goals — audience, brand tone, and technical feasibility — rather than defaulting to authority or personal preference.

---

## Question 10
**Question**: Your GRD designer is two days late on a deliverable. Your sprint review is in three days. What is the most professional approach?
**A)** Do nothing and hope the assets arrive in time
**B)** Send a professional check-in referencing the agreed timeline, ask for a revised ETA, and activate your backup plan (placeholder assets) so your sprint review can proceed regardless
**C)** Report the designer to their instructor immediately
**D)** Cancel your sprint review
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Proactive dependency management means checking in professionally, getting updated information, and activating contingency plans — your sprint should not be held hostage by a single dependency.

---

## Question 11
**Question**: Integration planning with a designer should happen:
**A)** After all design assets are delivered
**B)** Early and continuously — share technical specs upfront, test sample assets in code during iteration (not after final delivery), and surface format issues immediately
**C)** Only during the final sprint
**D)** Integration is not the designer's concern
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: Early integration testing catches format, size, and compatibility issues when they are cheap to fix — discovering that assets do not work in your codebase after final delivery wastes both parties' time.

---

## Question 12
**Question**: Which of the following is the best example of a "Waiting on Design" story in your sprint plan?
**A)** "Do design stuff"
**B)** "BLOCKED: Integrate navigation icons into header component. Dependency: Icon set delivery from GRD designer (expected Day 3). Unblocked work: Backend API endpoints, database schema. Escalation: If not received by Day 5, use placeholder icons and flag in standup."
**C)** "Wait for designer"
**D)** "Icons — TBD"
**Answer**: B
**Explanation**: A well-documented blocked story specifies the dependency, expected resolution date, alternative work to pursue while waiting, and an escalation trigger — this keeps the sprint productive despite the block.
