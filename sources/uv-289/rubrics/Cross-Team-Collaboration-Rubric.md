# Cross-Team Collaboration Rubric (75 pts total)

**Assignments**: M04-A2 (Design Brief Presentation, 25 pts) + M05-A2 (Design Feedback & Iteration, 25 pts) + M05-A3 (Design Asset Integration, 25 pts)
**Modules**: 4-5 (Weeks 6-8, GRD-242 collaboration phase)
**CLOs Assessed**: 2 (Cross-Team Collaboration), 3 (Design Asset Evaluation), 5 (Documentation)
**Context**: CSC-289 students collaborate with GRD-242 (Graphic Design IV) students under instructor Jennifer Fisher. These rubrics assess the quality of professional cross-functional collaboration, not the design quality itself.

---

## Component 1: Design Brief Presentation (25 pts) — M04-A2

**Format**: 15-minute presentation to GRD-242 class + written design brief document + post-presentation reflection
**When**: Module 4, Week 6

### Communication Professionalism (5 pts)

| Level | Description | Points |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| **Exemplary** | Presentation is well-structured with a clear beginning (project context), middle (design needs), and end (timeline and next steps). Speaker maintains eye contact, speaks at an appropriate pace, uses professional language free of unnecessary jargon, and stays within the 15-minute time limit. Written brief is polished, formatted professionally, and could be handed to a designer at a real company. | 5 |
| **Proficient** | Presentation is organized but could be tighter. Minor pacing issues or slightly over/under time (within 2 minutes of limit). Written brief is complete but has minor formatting or clarity gaps. Speaker occasionally lapses into technical jargon without translation. | 4 |
| **Developing** | Presentation is disorganized or significantly over/under time (3+ minutes). Speaker reads from notes extensively or turns back to screen frequently. Written brief is incomplete or poorly formatted. Audience must work to follow the narrative. | 3 |
| **Beginning** | Presentation is unprepared, significantly unprofessional, or not delivered. Speaker cannot articulate the project without reading a script verbatim. Written brief is missing. GRD-242 students cannot understand what they are being asked to design for. | 0-2 |

### Project Vision Clarity for Non-Technical Audience (5 pts)

| Level | Description | Points |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| **Exemplary** | Presenter communicates the project's purpose, target users, and core problem being solved in a way that a non-technical audience immediately understands. Technical concepts are translated into plain language (e.g., "the app helps students track study hours" rather than "a Flask CRUD application with SQLite persistence"). GRD-242 students could accurately describe the project to someone else after the presentation. | 5 |
| **Proficient** | Project purpose is communicated but some technical jargon remains unexplained, or the target user description is abstract rather than concrete. A designer could work from this but might need clarifying questions. | 4 |
| **Developing** | Presentation is code-focused rather than user-focused. GRD-242 students would struggle to understand what the project does or who it serves. Presenter talks about "the backend" and "the database" rather than what users see and do. | 3 |
| **Beginning** | Presentation fails to communicate the project's purpose to a non-technical audience. Audience is confused about what they are being asked to design for. No user-facing narrative established. | 0-2 |

### Technical Constraint Clarity (5 pts)

| Level | Description | Points |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| **Exemplary** | Technical constraints are clearly communicated in terms a designer can act on: accepted file formats (SVG, PNG, WebP), specific dimensions or aspect ratios, responsive breakpoints where assets must work, file size limits, color space requirements (sRGB vs. CMYK), accessibility requirements (WCAG AA contrast ratios), and any framework-specific constraints (e.g., "icons must be single-color SVGs for CSS theming"). Constraints are documented in the written brief with examples. | 5 |
| **Proficient** | Most relevant technical constraints are mentioned but not all. Constraints are stated clearly enough for a designer to follow, but 1-2 important constraints are missing (e.g., responsive behavior not addressed, or accessibility requirements omitted). Written brief documents constraints but without visual examples. | 4 |
| **Developing** | Technical constraints are mentioned in passing or only when the GRD instructor asks. Constraints are stated in developer jargon that designers cannot act on (e.g., "needs to work with flexbox" instead of "must scale from 320px to 1440px wide"). | 3 |
| **Beginning** | No technical constraints communicated. Designer has no guardrails for deliverables and will likely produce assets requiring significant rework. Student treats the designer as a "make it pretty" service rather than a collaborative partner. | 0-2 |

### Design Requirements Specificity (5 pts)

| Level | Description | Points |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| **Exemplary** | Design needs are itemized with specific deliverables: each request includes the asset name, dimensions or size range, context of use (where in the application it appears), desired style or mood (with reference images or style comparisons), and priority level. Presenter distinguishes between must-have and nice-to-have design assets. Written brief includes annotated screenshots or wireframes showing where each asset will live. | 5 |
| **Proficient** | Design needs are listed with some specifics but missing details a designer would need (e.g., "need a logo" without size, background context, or usage scenarios). Reference images or style direction provided for some but not all requests. | 4 |
| **Developing** | Design needs are vague ("make it look professional") or presented as a wish list rather than actionable requirements. No format, dimension, or context specifications. Designer would need a follow-up meeting to clarify every request. | 3 |
| **Beginning** | No specific design requirements communicated, or requests are unrealistic for the collaboration timeline (e.g., "redesign the entire UI" in one week). Student has not thought through what they actually need from a designer. | 0-2 |

### Live or Recorded Demo (5 pts)

| Level | Description | Points |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| **Exemplary** | Live or recorded demo shows the current state of the application with clear narration. Presenter highlights specific screens, components, or areas where design assets are needed, pointing to existing placeholder elements. Demo runs smoothly. Audience can see the "before" state that design will improve. | 5 |
| **Proficient** | Demo shows the application state but does not clearly connect current state to specific design needs. Minor technical difficulties that are recovered from gracefully. Placeholder elements exist but are not explicitly called out. | 4 |
| **Developing** | Demo is primarily screenshots or static mockups rather than working software. Design need areas are mentioned verbally but not shown visually. Audience cannot see the application in its current state. | 3 |
| **Beginning** | No demo provided, or demo is non-functional. GRD-242 students cannot see what they are designing for. Presentation is entirely abstract with no visual reference points. | 0-2 |

**Component 1 Total: 25 pts**

## Component 2: Design Feedback & Iteration (25 pts) — M05-A2

**Format**: Feedback documentation using SBI framework, iteration logs, communication evidence
**When**: Module 5, Week 7-8

### Feedback Quality Using SBI Framework (7 pts)

| Level | Description | Points |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| **Exemplary** | All feedback uses the Situation-Behavior-Impact (SBI) framework or equivalent structured approach. Feedback is specific and actionable, referencing the original design brief requirements (e.g., "Situation: The logo appears on the navbar at 40x40px. Behavior: The current version has thin line details that disappear at that size. Impact: Users cannot distinguish the logo from a generic icon, which weakens brand recognition. Suggestion: Simplify the mark to 3-4 bold shapes that read clearly at small sizes."). Positive aspects are acknowledged before change requests. Each asset receives individual, detailed feedback. | 7 |
| **Proficient** | Feedback is mostly specific and actionable, referencing original requirements, but does not consistently use the SBI framework. Some feedback is structured while other feedback is more informal. Positive aspects are acknowledged. Most assets receive individual feedback, but 1-2 may be addressed in a general comment. | 5-6 |
| **Developing** | Feedback is vague, subjective, or delivered as demands rather than collaborative requests (e.g., "I don't like the colors" or "redo the logo"). Missing acknowledgment of what works well. Feedback is based on personal preference rather than alignment with documented requirements. No structured framework applied. | 3-4 |
| **Beginning** | No substantive feedback provided to designer. Feedback is limited to "looks good" (rubber-stamping) or "redo everything" (no constructive direction). Student ghosted the designer after receiving initial assets. | 0-2 |

### Evaluation Against Acceptance Criteria (5 pts)

| Level | Description | Points |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| **Exemplary** | Student uses a structured evaluation framework (checklist, rubric, or pass/fail matrix) to evaluate each received design asset against the original design brief requirements. Each asset is evaluated individually with specific pass/fail criteria mapped to the brief. Framework is documented and shared with the designer so both parties understand the evaluation standard. Evaluation distinguishes between "does not meet requirements" and "meets requirements but could be improved." | 5 |
| **Proficient** | Student evaluates design assets against original requirements but without a formal documented framework. Evaluations are specific and reference the brief, but the process is informal. Framework exists internally but was not shared with the designer. | 4 |
| **Developing** | Evaluation is informal and subjective ("I like it" / "not what I wanted"). No structured criteria applied. No reference to the original design brief. Feedback cannot be traced back to documented requirements. | 2-3 |
| **Beginning** | No evaluation of received assets against any criteria. Student accepted everything without review or rejected everything without explanation. | 0-1 |

### Iteration Responsiveness (7 pts)

| Level | Description | Points |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| **Exemplary** | Student responds to designer questions and revised deliverables within 24 hours. Multiple feedback-revision cycles are documented (2+ rounds). Student adapts requirements when the designer identifies legitimate constraints (e.g., "That animation would require JavaScript integration — here is a CSS-only alternative that achieves a similar effect"). Communication log shows a professional back-and-forth cadence. Student proactively checks in if designer is silent for 48+ hours. | 7 |
| **Proficient** | Responses are within 48 hours. At least one complete revision cycle is documented. Student is generally responsive but had 1-2 communication gaps. Student accommodates designer constraints when raised but does not proactively anticipate them. | 5-6 |
| **Developing** | Response times are inconsistent (48+ hours for some communications). Only one round of feedback occurred (initial delivery accepted or rejected with no iteration). Student did not adapt when the designer raised constraints, insisting on the original specification. | 3-4 |
| **Beginning** | Student was unresponsive to the designer for extended periods (72+ hours), or no iteration occurred. Student accepted the first draft without review, or rejected deliverables without offering revision opportunity. No evidence of collaborative iteration. | 0-2 |

### Communication Professionalism & Documentation (6 pts)

| Level | Description | Points |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| **Exemplary** | All communication is logged with dates, medium (email, Slack, Teams, in-person), content summary, and action items with owners. Communication log demonstrates a professional cadence (2+ substantive touchpoints per week during active collaboration). Tone is consistently respectful, collaborative, and appreciative. Student initiates communication rather than only responding. | 6 |
| **Proficient** | Communication log exists with most interactions documented. Some gaps in timestamps or content summaries. Cadence is adequate (1-2 touchpoints per week). Tone is professional. Student is responsive but does not always initiate. | 4-5 |
| **Developing** | Communication log is sparse or reconstructed after the fact (dates approximate, content summaries vague). Significant gaps in documentation. Cadence suggests minimal interaction (fewer than 1 touchpoint per week). | 2-3 |
| **Beginning** | No communication log, or log shows fewer than 3 total interactions across the collaboration period. No evidence of professional communication practices. Student treated the collaboration as a transactional handoff rather than an ongoing partnership. | 0-1 |

**Component 2 Total: 25 pts**

## Component 3: Design Asset Integration (25 pts) — M05-A3

**Format**: Integration PRs with acceptance tests, designer attribution, collaboration closure
**When**: Module 5, Week 8

### Integration Quality (8 pts)

| Level | Description | Points |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| **Exemplary** | Design assets are integrated into the working application with proper file organization (assets in a logical directory structure, not dumped in root), responsive scaling across breakpoints, and cross-browser compatibility (Chrome, Firefox, Safari minimum). Assets render as intended in context. CSS/styling adjustments are minimal and documented. No broken images, layout shifts, or accessibility regressions. Integration PR(s) are clean with descriptive commit messages. | 8 |
| **Proficient** | Assets are integrated and functional but with minor issues (e.g., slight scaling problems on one breakpoint, one browser has a minor display inconsistency). Most assets look as intended. File organization is reasonable. PR exists with adequate description. | 6-7 |
| **Developing** | Assets are added to the repository but not properly integrated into the UI. Placeholder elements remain alongside new assets. Significant styling issues (wrong size, broken layout, overlapping elements). Integration is incomplete or creates visual regressions elsewhere in the application. | 4-5 |
| **Beginning** | Assets are not integrated, are in the wrong formats, or break existing functionality when added. Repository contains raw, unprocessed design files with no integration work. No PR created for integration. | 0-3 |

### Acceptance Testing Rigor (7 pts)

| Level | Description | Points |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| **Exemplary** | Each integrated asset is tested against the original acceptance criteria from the design brief. Test results are documented in a structured format (pass/fail per criterion per asset). Edge cases are tested: dark mode (if applicable), mobile viewport, slow connection (image loading), keyboard navigation, and screen reader compatibility. Issues found during testing are logged as GitHub Issues with severity labels. Test evidence includes screenshots or screen recordings demonstrating pass/fail states. | 7 |
| **Proficient** | Assets are tested against most acceptance criteria from the brief. Testing is documented but does not cover edge cases (e.g., only tested on desktop Chrome). Issues found are noted in the PR description or integration document but not formally tracked as GitHub Issues. | 5-6 |
| **Developing** | Minimal testing performed. Student confirms "it looks right" through visual inspection without systematic verification against acceptance criteria. No documentation of test process or results. Testing is "I opened it in my browser and it looked fine." | 3-4 |
| **Beginning** | No acceptance testing evidence. Assets were dropped into the codebase and committed without any verification. Broken assets or visual regressions are present in the merged code. | 0-2 |

### Designer Attribution (5 pts)

| Level | Description | Points |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| **Exemplary** | Designer is credited in the project README (Contributors section with name, role, and course context), in code comments near asset usage (e.g., `/* Logo design by [Name], GRD-242, FTCC Spring 2026 */`), and in any public-facing about/credits page of the deployed application. Attribution includes designer's name, specific contribution description, and GRD-242 course context. Designer was informed of the attribution format and approved it before publication. | 5 |
| **Proficient** | Designer is credited in the README and at least one other location (code comments or about page). Attribution is accurate and includes the designer's name and general contribution, but may lack specifics (e.g., "design assets" rather than "logo system, color palette, and icon set"). Designer was informed but approval not explicitly documented. | 4 |
| **Developing** | Designer is credited in only one location (e.g., just the README) or attribution is generic ("thanks to my designer" without naming them). No code comments or public-facing credit. Designer may not have been informed of how they were credited. | 2-3 |
| **Beginning** | No designer attribution anywhere in the project. Designer's contribution is completely unacknowledged. This is professionally unacceptable and would constitute an ethics violation in a workplace context. | 0-1 |

### Collaboration Closure Professionalism (5 pts)

| Level | Description | Points |
|-------|-------------|--------|
| **Exemplary** | Student sends a formal thank-you communication to the designer summarizing what was accomplished together, sharing the deployed result (URL or screenshots), highlighting how the design assets improved the application, and offering to serve as a portfolio reference or LinkedIn connection. Collaboration retrospective in the sprint retro documents specific lessons learned for future cross-team work. Closure communication is sent within 1 week of final integration. | 5 |
| **Proficient** | Student thanks the designer and shares the final result, but closure is informal (e.g., brief Slack message rather than substantive thank-you). Retrospective mentions the GRD-242 collaboration with some reflection on what was learned. | 4 |
| **Developing** | Collaboration ends without formal closure. No thank-you or result-sharing with the designer. Retrospective mentions GRD-242 in passing without substantive reflection on the collaboration experience. | 2-3 |
| **Beginning** | No closure communication sent. Student disengaged from the collaboration without professional conclusion. Designer does not know the outcome of their work. No reflection on the collaboration experience in any course artifact. | 0-1 |

**Component 3 Total: 25 pts**

## Instructor Notes on Grading Collaboration

- **Do not penalize students for designer quality.** Evaluate the CSC-289 student's communication, feedback, and integration process, not whether the GRD-242 student produced excellent design work.
- **Do penalize students for poor collaboration behavior.** Late responses, vague feedback, no iteration, missing attribution, and ghosting are all within the CSC-289 student's control.
- **If a GRD-242 student drops or is unresponsive**: Work with the student to document the situation, adjust expectations, and grade based on the process the student followed (e.g., communication attempts, contingency plan execution, placeholder asset strategy).
- **Collaboration evidence should be verifiable**: Communication logs should include screenshots of actual messages, not just summaries. If using Slack or email, students should export relevant threads.
