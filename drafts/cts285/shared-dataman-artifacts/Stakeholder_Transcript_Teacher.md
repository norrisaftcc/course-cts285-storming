**Stakeholder:** Ms. Alvarez — 3rd-grade classroom teacher (24 students)
**Role in project:** Classroom adopter / power curator
**Interview length:** ~15 minutes (excerpt)

---

**Interviewer:** How would something like this fit into your classroom?

**Ms. Alvarez:** If it works the way I hope, it's a math-facts station. I rotate small groups through stations during our math block, and fluency drill is the one that's hardest to make engaging. Worksheets are dead on arrival. If the kids *want* to pick it up, that's already a win.

**Interviewer:** What would make it useful specifically for you, versus for a parent at home?

**Ms. Alvarez:** Scale and control. I have twenty-four kids and they are not all in the same place. Some are still shaky on addition facts; a few are ready for multiplication. I need to load different problem sets for different kids — or at least different groups — and I need to do it fast, because I'm setting up five stations at 7:40 in the morning. The old DataMan let a teacher store a set of problems for a child to work. I need that, but for a whole class, per kid.

**Interviewer:** So you'd curate targeted sets, assigned to individual students.

**Ms. Alvarez:** Right. And here's a thing I love from the original that people forget — it let kids "play teacher." They could make up problems *for each other*. That's not busywork, that's real learning. The kid who builds the problem is thinking harder than the kid who solves it. I'd want students to be able to author problems for a partner, not just receive them.

**Interviewer:** Let's talk about what you'd want to see as the teacher.

**Ms. Alvarez:** Reporting. But careful reporting. I want to see, across the class, *which facts are the sticky ones* — if eighteen kids are blowing the seven-times table, that changes my whole-group lesson tomorrow. So I need it down to the individual attempt: not just "she got 7 out of 10," but *which* ones, and whether she got it on the first try or the second. First-try versus second-try tells me the difference between "knows it" and "guessed it."

**Interviewer:** The device gives two tries per problem and records right-versus-tried. You'd want that attempt-level detail preserved and surfaced.

**Ms. Alvarez:** Yes. The score summary is fine for the kid. *I* need the granular version.

**Interviewer:** One thing the manual is firm about: DataMan won't do negative numbers. If a subtraction would go below zero, it just refuses the entry.

**Ms. Alvarez:** Good. Keep that. My third-graders haven't seen negatives and shouldn't in this context — it would just confuse them. Honestly that "quirk" is grade-appropriate scoping and I'd defend it. Same with keeping the problems to small numbers. The original kept operands to one or two digits, and that's right for this age.

**Interviewer:** There are also timed games — Electro Flash, Wipe Out — where a clock runs and kids race it.

**Ms. Alvarez:** Mmm. Careful there. Some kids thrive on the timer and some fall apart. I have a couple of kids who go blank the second a clock starts. I'd want the drill part to be available *without* the race, so I can turn the pressure off for the kids who need it off. The games are great for the kids who like them. They're not for everyone.

**Interviewer:** A parent I spoke with wants a class leaderboard — public ranking of top scorers.

**Ms. Alvarez:** No. Absolutely not. I will not put a ranking board up in my room. The whole reason a tool like this works is that a struggling kid gets to struggle *privately* and still feel the little win. The moment you rank them against each other, the kids at the bottom stop trying — those are exactly the kids I'm trying to reach. Measure each kid against where *they* were last week. Never against each other. That's a hill I'll die on.

**Interviewer:** Understood — that's a real tension I'll flag. Who should be able to see a given student's data?

**Ms. Alvarez:** I see all of my students', obviously. That's just... I'm their teacher.

**Interviewer:** And a parent seeing their own child's?

**Ms. Alvarez:** Sure, I guess, their own. Anyway — as long as it's aligned to our standards, I'm interested. Just make it an app on the class iPads and I'll pilot it.

**Interviewer:** When you say "aligned to our standards" — which standards, specifically?

**Ms. Alvarez:** Our math fluency benchmarks. The district ones. I'd have to pull the exact document, but you know — the grade-level fluency expectations. It should hit those.

**Interviewer:** We'll need to pin that down. Thank you, this is very useful.
