> **Project context:** You are a systems analyst on the *Dataman modernization initiative* — rebuilding Texas Instruments' 1977 handheld math toy *DataMan* as a modern web/app learning system. Device behavior in this transcript traces to the transcribed manual (`reference/dataman/DATAMAN_MANUAL_TRANSCRIPT.md`). The stakeholder's *wishes* are their own; some are good requirements, some are not.

**Stakeholder:** Renee — parent of a 7-year-old (2nd grade)
**Role in project:** Primary buyer / at-home supervising adult
**Interview length:** ~15 minutes (excerpt)

---

**Interviewer:** Thanks for making time. Tell me how math practice works at home right now.

**Renee:** Honestly, it's a fight. Tomas is in second grade and he's fine at math *in general*, but there are a handful of facts that just will not stick. Six times six. Seven plus six. Every single time, he freezes on those exact ones. We've tried flash cards and he just gets frustrated and shuts down.

**Interviewer:** So it's specific facts, not math in general.

**Renee:** Exactly. It's like four or five problems that trip him every time. If the thing could just drill *those* — the ones he keeps missing — and leave the rest alone, that would be the whole ballgame for me. I don't need it to teach him everything. I need it to hammer his weak spots.

**Interviewer:** The original DataMan actually had a feature for that — a "memory bank" where a parent could store up to ten problems for the child to practice.

**Renee:** See, that's perfect. That's exactly it. I want to be able to punch in the five or six facts he's struggling with this week, hand him the thing, and know he's practicing *those*. And then next week when they change, I swap them out.

**Interviewer:** When he practices, what do you want to happen when he gets one wrong?

**Renee:** Do not make him feel stupid. That's rule one. When I was a kid I had this DataMan, actually — my dad's, an old one — and the thing I remember is it never made you feel bad. You got two tries, and if you missed it, it just showed you the right answer and moved on. It never like... flashed the wrong answer at you like it was real. It just gave you a little light show when you did well. He needs that. He needs the win, not the red X.

**Interviewer:** So: two attempts, then reveal the correct answer, and a reward when he does well.

**Renee:** Yes. And make the reward feel like a reward. The light show mattered to me as a kid. I still remember it.

**Interviewer:** How do you want to know how he's doing? Do you want to watch him practice?

**Renee:** No — that's the point, I *can't* hover, I've got a toddler too. I want to check later and see how he did. Like, "he practiced today, he got 8 out of 10, here are the two he still missed." If I can see the ones he's still missing, I know what to put in for next week. But I don't want to sit there the whole time.

**Interviewer:** Got it. Anything about how long he uses it?

**Renee:** It should not be a screen he disappears into. The old one shut itself off if you walked away for a few minutes — I liked that. I don't want this to become another thing I have to pry out of his hands. Short sessions. When he wanders off, it should just... stop.

**Interviewer:** Let me ask about competition. Some parents want their kids to see how they rank against classmates.

**Renee:** Oh, you should absolutely put in a leaderboard. Show him where he ranks against his whole class — that would light a fire under him. Tomas is competitive, he'd practice for hours if he could beat the other kids. Put the top scorers up on a board so everyone can see. That's the number-one thing that would get him to actually use it.

**Interviewer:** Interesting — I'll note that. Last thing: is this his own, or shared?

**Renee:** It's his. He'd have his own login, his own stuff, his scores. Obviously. Each kid has their own account — that's just how apps work now, right?

**Interviewer:** We'll look at that. Anything else you'd want?

**Renee:** Just... make it safe, and make it good. You know what I mean? Like the old one was. I trusted it. I want to trust this one.

**Interviewer:** That's really helpful, Renee. Thank you.
